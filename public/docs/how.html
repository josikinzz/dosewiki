How dose.wiki articles are made

the full content pipeline — scraping, excerpting, synthesis, human review, citations

A dose.wiki substance article is put together from several distinct bodies of work: subjective effects built on the documentation of Josie Kins, interaction ratings from TripSit's combination research, live reagent test results from protestkit.eu, structured data parsed from reference sites, and prose sections synthesized from archival source material. A human expert reviewer is the final gate before anything is published. This page documents the whole process end to end, including the exact prompts we use.

01 Why this page exists

This page is linked from the source pages section of every substance article. It is one shared explanation of where article content comes from, not a per-article audit log. Different parts of an article come from different places:

Subjective effects

Built on the subjective effects documentation work of Josie Kins. Her effect taxonomy is the basis of our Subjective Effect Index, which every effect listing links into.

Interactions

Sourced from TripSit's drug combination research. Their combination chart provides the risk ratings shown on every article.

Reagent tests

Sourced live from protestkit.eu. Color reactions are fetched from their API when you view an article.

The other sections

Synthesized from archival source material: ten reference and harm-reduction sites, processed through the parsing, excerpting, and writing stages this page describes.

Two rules hold across all of it:

Every article is human-reviewed

No article is shown to the public until a human expert reviewer has manually read and approved it: every article, every time, as a matter of principle. AI output is treated as a draft for review, never as something that publishes on its own. Section 09 describes the review process in detail.

Every claim is source-bound

Models are given only verbatim excerpts of the source material to write from, under prompts that forbid claims not present in those excerpts. These constraints are written to reinforce grounded behavior, not assumed to guarantee it. The per-claim citations (section 08) and the final review exist to catch what slips through.

02 The pipeline at a glance

Source pages are gathered once as internal working copies, then flow through two parallel tracks: a deterministic track that parses structured data such as dosage tables and interaction matrices, and an AI track that turns the prose parts of the sources into excerpt documents and then into draft sections. Both tracks fill a draft article, which then receives per-claim citations and, last of all, a human expert review, the final gate before anything is published.

05 Stage one — scraping & parsing

The source sites were scraped once and stored as plain-text working copies, per substance: internal processing material, not something we host or republish. From there, deterministic parsers (ordinary code, one per source site, no AI involved) read that text and extract the structured data: dose ranges by route, duration values, interaction ratings, chemical identifiers, legal classifications. Each site needs its own parser because each site structures its pages differently.

Table parsers

TripSit factsheets and PsychonautWiki carry the most structured data: dose tiers (threshold / light / common / strong / heavy) per route of administration and onset / peak / offset durations. The parsers read these tables directly and transcribe the numbers as published. When multiple sources give different doses for the same route, we take the smallest as authoritative, a deliberate safety bias on the assumption that underdosing is the safer error.

The combination chart

The TripSit combination guide is ingested as structured data and mapped onto substance pairs to produce the interaction ratings (dangerous, unsafe, caution, low-risk) shown on every article and on the Interactions hub.

Chemistry identifiers

SMILES strings, InChI keys, CAS numbers, and molecular data are merged from DrugBank, PsychonautWiki, and Wikipedia, with provenance tracked per identifier and chemical-name resolution used to fill gaps. Molecule images are rendered from the SMILES strings.

Prose-heavy sources

Erowid, Isomer Design, SaferParty, The Drug Classroom, Disregard Everything I Say, and the Drug Users Bible contribute mostly narrative text. Their parsers identify and label the relevant passages, which then feed the excerpt-extraction stage below.

06 Stage two — verbatim excerpt extraction

The source articles are long, and most of any given page is irrelevant to any one section. So before any writing happens, an AI pass goes through every source article and copies out the passages relevant to each topic, word for word. The extraction prompts forbid paraphrasing: the output must be verbatim quotations, complete paragraphs, with formatting preserved, organized under a heading for each source. The result is one excerpt document per substance per topic, one for each of the AI-written sections:

The excerpt topics
  • Intro text (feeds the summary)
  • Pharmacology
  • Tolerance
  • Harm potential
  • History & culture
  • Legality
Why verbatim matters

The excerpt documents are the only material the writing stage is allowed to see. Because they are word-for-word copies, our reviewers can trace anything a draft says back to a specific passage on a specific source page during review. Extraction errs toward over-inclusion: the instruction is to include anything even tangentially relevant rather than risk losing safety-critical information.

Extraction prompt — intro text 
# Intro Text Extraction Prompt

You are extracting introductory and summary content from source articles about a psychoactive substance.

## Task
Extract verbatim text that describes WHAT the substance IS - its identity, classification, and basic characteristics. This is the "encyclopedia-style" introduction content.

## What to Extract

### Basic Identity
- Full chemical name and common names
- Street names and slang
- Drug class/category (psychedelic, stimulant, depressant, dissociative, etc.)
- Chemical family (tryptamine, phenethylamine, lysergamide, benzodiazepine, etc.)
- Brief description of what the substance is

### General Characteristics
- Physical appearance (color, form, taste, smell)
- Common forms available (powder, crystal, blotter, pill, plant material)
- Routes of administration (brief mention, NOT detailed dosing)
- General potency statements
- Comparison to related substances

### Brief Context
- One-sentence mentions of discovery (NOT detailed history)
- One-sentence legal status summary (NOT detailed legality)
- General use context (recreational, therapeutic, traditional)

## What NOT to Extract
These belong in OTHER extraction categories:

| Category | Goes To |
|----------|---------|
| Detailed dosage tiers | dosage-duration |
| Duration/onset timing | dosage-duration |
| Mechanism of action | pharmacology |
| Half-life, bioavailability | pharmacology |
| Receptor binding details | pharmacology |
| Metabolism details | pharmacology |
| Legal status by country | legality |
| Historical timeline | history-culture |
| Detailed discovery narrative | history-culture |
| Subjective effects lists | subjective-effects |
| Physical/cognitive effects | subjective-effects |
| Tolerance information | tolerance |

## Extraction Rules
1. Extract COMPLETE paragraphs - do not truncate mid-sentence
2. Preserve markdown formatting (headers, lists, bold, etc.)
3. Be SELECTIVE - only include content that describes what the substance IS
4. If a paragraph mixes intro with detailed effects/dosage, extract only the intro portion
5. Intro content is typically 1-4 paragraphs per source
6. Do NOT paraphrase - extract verbatim text only

## Where to Find Intro Content
- Opening paragraphs after the title
- "Overview" or "Summary" sections
- "Description" or "About" sections
- Lead paragraphs in Wikipedia-style articles
- Brief descriptions in factsheet headers

## Output Format

For each source that has intro content, output:

```
## Source: {Source Name}

{verbatim extracted text}

---
```

If a source has NO intro content, output:

```
## Source: {Source Name}

*No intro content found.*

---
```

## Schema Field Mapping
When extracting, look for content that answers:
- `name` - What is the common name?
- `aliases` - What are the street names/slang?
- `drug_class` - What type of drug is this?
- `chemical_class` - What chemical family?
- `description` - 1-2 sentence summary
- `appearance` - What does it look like?
- `common_forms` - Powder, pill, blotter, etc.
- `routes` - How is it typically taken?

## Important
Keep intro extractions concise and focused. This content will be used for the opening section of articles - users want to quickly understand what a substance is before diving into details.

07 Stage three — AI synthesis, and every prompt we use

Six sections of the finished article are AI-written: summary, pharmacology, tolerance, harm potential, history & culture, and legality. Each is produced by Claude Opus 4.5 from exactly two inputs: the excerpt document for that topic, and a section-specific prompt. The prompts all share the same skeleton: a style definition, a hard evidence rule, a fixed output format, worked examples, and a validation checklist. They all contain two constraints that define the whole system:

Evidence only

"Every claim must be explicitly stated or clearly implied in the source quotes. If information cannot be verified from the provided sources, do not include it." This pushes the model to behave as a rewriter of evidence rather than an author. It is reinforcement, not a guarantee, which is why the citation pass and human review come after.

Original prose only

"Synthesize information from the provided excerpts into original prose. Do not copy sentences or lengthy phrases verbatim from any source." The verbatim text exists only in the internal excerpt documents; what gets published is a new piece of writing constrained by that evidence.

#### Findings Field - CRITICAL GUIDANCE

The `findings` field is displayed prominently in collapsed organ toxicity cards. It must be:

1. **A complete sentence** (not a comma-separated list of terms)
2. **Self-contained** - readable and informative without expanding for more details
3. **Contextualized** - ALWAYS specify under what conditions the damage occurs:
   - "with chronic heavy use"
   - "at doses significantly exceeding typical recreational ranges"
   - "with prolonged daily use over months/years"
   - "primarily in cases of addiction/compulsive use"
   - "at extreme/overdose-level doses"

4. **Proportionate** - Do NOT over-exaggerate. If damage only occurs with extreme abuse patterns, say so clearly. Users should understand these are not risks from occasional responsible use.

Reference standards
  • Wikis are never citations. Wikipedia and PsychonautWiki are used to discover references, but the references themselves are journals, government documents, drug labels, and books.

In our own words, lightly adapted from the About page:

dose.wiki exists to serve as a modern, open-source, and comprehensive informational harm reduction resource for psychoactive substances and their subjective effects. The motivation for creating it was simply that the various overlapping subcultures of the psychonaut, psychedelic, and drugnerd communities have been sorely lacking a resource of this kind for far too long — a website that is up to modern web standards and is open-source, with an easily accessible GitHub repository.

Our hope for this platform is that it can serve not just as a convenient and helpful application, but also as a comprehensive database of consistently structured data that is so easy for researchers and developers to download, build on, and fork that nobody ever has to go through the effort of researching and constructing an encyclopedia of this kind from scratch ever again. It's 2026+ already and we are living in the future; we think it's about time that this knowledge stopped being gatekept behind aging platforms and disparate APIs.

That intention is why the pipeline looks the way it does. Documentation at this scale has historically required either institutional funding or years of volunteer labor. The volunteer projects that carried that weight (several of them our sources) also show what it costs: coverage gaps, aging software, knowledge trapped in page markup. The dataset is the deliverable as much as the website is. If dose.wiki vanished tomorrow, the work wouldn't.
