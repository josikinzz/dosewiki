## Source: DrugBank

### Toxicity

**Oral LD50**: 427 mg kg-1 (rat)

### Overdose/Toxicity

**Symptoms of opioid toxicity** may include confusion, somnolence, shallow breathing, constricted pupils, nausea, vomiting, constipation and a lack of appetite. In severe cases, **symptoms of circulatory and respiratory depression may ensue, which may be life-threatening or fatal**.

### Respiratory Depression

**Codeine also causes sedation, drowsiness, and respiratory depression**. **Codeine may cause respiratory system depression by the activation of μ-opioid receptors at specific sites in the central nervous system**.

### Pregnancy & Neonatal Effects

**Teratogenic effects**: This drug is classified as a pregnancy Category C drug.

Codeine has shown **embryolethal and fetotoxic effects in the hamster, rat as well as mouse models at about 2-4 times the maximum recommended human dose**.

**Nonteratogenic effects**: **Neonatal codeine withdrawal has been observed in infants born to addicted and non-addicted mothers who ingested codeine-containing medications in the days before delivery.** Common symptoms of narcotic withdrawal include irritability, excessive crying, tremors, hyperreflexia, seizures, fever, vomiting, diarrhea, and poor feeding.

Codeine (30 mg/kg) given subcutaneously to pregnant rats during gestation and for 25 days after delivery **increased the rate of neonatal mortality at birth**.

### Breastfeeding Risk

**Codeine is secreted into human milk. The maternal use of codeine can potentially lead to serious adverse reactions, including death, in nursing infants**.

### Drug Interactions

**The risk or severity of adverse effects can be increased when Codeine is combined with benzodiazepines.**

**The serum concentration of Codeine can be increased** when combined with various CYP2D6 inhibitors.

### Food Interactions

**Avoid alcohol.**

## Source: Drug Users Bible

### Adverse Effects

**Possible side effects** include drowsiness and constipation, whilst less frequently, **conditions such as itching, nausea, dry mouth and urinary retention can arise.**

### Addiction and Overdose Risk

**As with other opioids, addiction is a serious threat, as is the risk of overdose.** A further risk is sometimes presented with codeine in that it is often supplied in combination with paracetamol, ibuprofen, or similar. **At high doses these in themselves can cause serious health issues and even death. Please do not brush these warnings aside.**

## Source: Safer Party

### Short-term Risks

**Dry mouth, headache, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, itching, constipation, difficulty in urinating, tiredness/sleepiness, decrease in respiratory rate up to a life-threatening respiratory depression.** In men, erectile dysfunction may also occur.

**There is a risk of psychological and physical dependence.** In the case of chronic use, tolerance may develop, so that the dose must be progressively increased to achieve the desired effect.

**When discontinuing codeine after a phase of continuous use, withdrawal symptoms such as restlessness, stomach and/or leg cramps, cold shivers, trembling, profuse sweating and muscle spasms may appear.** Due to the high dependence potential, **withdrawal can be as painful and long-lasting as heroin withdrawal (!).**

### Long-term Risks

In men, **there may be a reduction in sexual desire (libido).** In women, **menstruation may be absent or irregular and sexual desire may be reduced.**

**Other long-term risks include allergic reactions, sleep disturbances and dizziness.** **The dependence potential of codeine is lower than that of other opiates, but should still not be underestimated!**

### Drug Interactions & Safer Use

**Do not use codeine with other depressant substances such as alcohol, cannabis, sleeping pills, MAO inhibitors (such as antidepressants) or GHB/GBL.**

**When codeine is injected or snorted, severe side effects and unintentional overdose can result.**

**Codeine is a medicinal product and should therefore only be taken with medical supervision.**

## Source: The Drug Classroom

### Respiratory Depression

**Codeine may begin to impact respiration** at or near medical doses, "albeit not in a dangerous way."

**Some medical institutions have switched to only giving codeine to children 12-and-older. It has occasionally caused severe respiratory depression and death at medical doses in young children.**

It is specifically **contraindicated in the postoperative period following tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy for obstructive sleep apnea**.

**Using too much, especially in people with a UM status, could result in fatal respiratory depression. It reliably alters respiration in a manner that can become easily dangerous when other depressants are co-administered.**

Studies comparing respiratory effects: "While codeine 60 mg led to less respiratory depression than morphine 10 mg, codeine 120 mg produced more respiratory depression than morphine 10 mg."

### Pediatric Deaths

FDA Adverse Event Reporting System data shows: **"10 cases of pediatric death and 3 cases of respiratory depression associated with the therapeutic use of codeine"** from 1969 to May 2012, with age range of 21 months to 9 years, majority in postoperative period after adenoidectomy or adenotonsillectomy.

**3 additional pediatric deaths** documented by Friedrichsdorf (2013):
- 10-year-old who underwent orthopedic surgery
- 4-year-old after tonsillectomy
- Child who received codeine as an antitussive at supratherapeutic dose

FDA Adverse Event Reporting System from 1965 to 2015 shows: **"64 cases of severe respiratory depression" and "24 codeine-related deaths"** with 21 involving children under 12-years-old.

From a review of pediatric malpractice claims: **"18% of deaths and 8% of nonfatal cases of opioid toxicity could be attributed to opioid use, most often codeine."**

**Fatal case involving medical doses** detailed: A 6-year-old female with history of myocarditis developed severe respiratory depression. She was found "a little blue" at 19:45. Next morning at 08:00, the mother found her daughter "lifeless, pale, and cold."

### Breastfeeding Deaths

**Breastfed baby died from respiratory depression.** The mother had three functional CYP2D6 alleles, making her an ultrarapid metabolizer, with analysis showing a **high morphine content in breast milk**.

Another case: **"1-month-old received combination medications including codeine as an antitussive and developed severe respiratory depression."**

### Overdose

**An overdose can come with respiratory depression, unconsciousness, coma, and vomit aspiration.**

**Death, though relatively rare, occurs via respiratory depression. Ultrarapid metabolizers are at a higher risk of fatal consequences.**

### Addiction & Dependence

**Physical dependence reliably develops with extended use.** Withdrawal symptoms include insomnia, restlessness, runny nose, stomach pain, diarrhea, and chills, which can "feel similar to a severe flu and sometimes worse."

In India study (Mattoo, 1997): Of 46 people with codeine-containing cough syrup dependence, **92% reported a withdrawal syndrome**, typically with onset of 4-48 hours (70% within 4-14 hours), lasting 1-5 days (75% lasting 4-5 days).

Historical note (Himmelsbach, 1934): Codeine withdrawal produced **"severe symptoms in all cases"** during the 4th-5th day after abrupt cessation, comparable to morphine but with delayed onset.

### Long-term Complications from Overuse

From Ireland study: **Acute tended to involve urticarial itching, distorted vision, and respiratory depression. Chronic centered around nausea, constipation, liver/bowel/kidney failure, anemia, seizures, ulcers, and swollen stomach.**

### Lethal Dose

Gonzales (1937) **estimated the lethal dose as 4.5 to 8 grains (291 to 518 mg)** though they also mentioned reports of recovery from larger amounts.

One documented case: A 21-year-old male who ingested three bottles of Robitussin AC **"would have approached the alleged minimum lethal dose of 800 mg, though alcohol and pheniramine could have played a role."**

### Drug Interactions

Studies show: **Alcohol, diazepam, and codeine led to increased risks during driving in emergency and monotonous surroundings.**

Alcohol combined with codeine resulted in drivers commonly **neglecting instructions and driving off the road, and causing more collisions.**

### Psychiatric Effects

**Codeine is considered more likely than some alternative opioids** to cause depression, mania, and even psychotic states, especially in overdose. There have been case reports of **manic episodes, auditory hallucinations, and paranoid delusions** following high-dose codeine use.

**Patients prescribed only codeine for greater than 30 days had a 29% increased risk of a new depression diagnosis** compared to those given hydrocodone.

### Seizures

Multiple documented cases of **seizures in patients with renal insufficiency** after codeine use, with seizure activity attributed to **hypoxia from respiratory depression**.

### Hepatotoxicity

**Though it's not really a concern in clinical settings, in vitro tests suggest there's some potential for hepatotoxicity.** Administration of codeine led to **dose- and time-dependent LDH leakage out of cells**, indicating cytotoxicity.

### Combination Product Risks

**Combinations, such as with paracetamol or ibuprofen, come with a higher risk of GI hemorrhage, nephrotoxicity, hypokalemia, acute hemorrhagic necrotizing pancreatitis, and brain damage.**

One documented case: 27 patients in Australia with misuse of OTC codeine-ibuprofen had **opioid dependence and GI complications**, with 3 cases of hypokalemia and 1 requiring dialysis.

### Pediatric Contraindications

- **April 2015** – European Medicines Agency said codeine **must not be used** for cough in children under 12.
- **July 2015** – FDA stated codeine **shouldn't be used as an antitussive in children under 18** due to potential for serious problems, including slowed or difficult breathing.
- **December 2015** – FDA advisory panel **overwhelmingly recommended the use of codeine for cough be contraindicated in all children under 18-years-old.**

**The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists age under 12 years old as a contraindication to use.**

### Purple Drank Deaths

**At least two members of the Texas hip-hop community, Robert Davis Jr (DJ Screw) and Chad Butler (Pimp C), died of causes partly attributable to purple drank.** Davis died in 2000 and Butler died in 2007.

**There's a tendency to underestimate its harm potential.**

## Source: Wikipedia

### Side Effects

**Common adverse effects** include drowsiness and constipation. Less common are itching, nausea, vomiting, dry mouth, miosis, orthostatic hypotension, urinary retention, euphoria, and dysphoria. Rare adverse effects include anaphylaxis, seizure, acute pancreatitis, and **respiratory depression**.

**Serious side effects** may include breathing difficulties and addiction.

### Respiratory Depression & Overdose

**As with other opioids, a potentially serious adverse drug reaction is respiratory depression.** This depression is dose-related and is a mechanism for the potentially fatal consequences of overdose. **As codeine is metabolized to morphine, morphine can be passed through breast milk in potentially lethal amounts, fatally depressing the respiration of a breastfed baby.**

**Life-threatening intoxication, including respiratory depression requiring intubation, can develop over a matter of days in patients who have multiple functional alleles of CYP2D6, resulting in ultrarapid metabolism of opioids such as codeine into morphine.**

### Pediatric Deaths & FDA Warnings

**In August 2012, the United States Food and Drug Administration issued a warning about deaths in pediatric patients less than 6 years old after ingesting "normal" doses of paracetamol with codeine after tonsillectomy; this warning was upgraded to a black box warning in February 2013.**

**Some patients are very effective converters of codeine to its active form, morphine, resulting in lethal blood levels.**

### CYP2D6 Metabolism Issues

**Due to the increased metabolism of codeine to morphine, ultrarapid metabolizers (those possessing more than two functional copies of the CYP2D6 allele) are at increased risk of adverse drug effects related to morphine toxicity.**

**However, CYP2D6 has been implicated in the toxicity and death of neonates when codeine is administered to lactating mothers, particularly those with increased enzyme activity ("ultra-rapid" metabolizers).**

### Breastfeeding & Toxicity

Care should be used during breastfeeding, **as it may result in opiate toxicity in the baby.**

### Addiction, Dependence & Withdrawal

**Codeine exhibits abuse potential similar to other opioid medications, including a risk of addiction and overdose.**

**As with other opiates, chronic use of codeine can cause physical dependence which can lead to severe withdrawal symptoms if a person suddenly stops the medication.** Withdrawal symptoms include drug craving, runny nose, yawning, sweating, insomnia, weakness, stomach cramps, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle spasms, chills, irritability, and pain.

**In 2019 Ireland was said to be on the verge of a codeine addiction epidemic, according to a paper in the Irish Medical Journal.**

### Contraindications & Age Restrictions

**The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) lists age under 12 years old as a contraindication to use.** The American Academy of Pediatrics does not recommend its use in children due to side effects.

**Its use as of 2016 is not recommended in children.**

### Ethiopian Population Risk

**Approximately 30% of the Ethiopian population carry an extra copy of the gene CYP2D6, and are classified as codeine ultrametabolizers. These individuals metabolize codeine to morphine at a dangerously fast rate, leading to adverse events and potentially death.** As a consequence the Ethiopian Food, Medicine and Health Care Administration and Control Authority has entirely banned the use of codeine as unsafe for the general population.

### Recreational Use & Deaths

**Rapper Pimp C, from the group UGK, died from an overdose of this combination** (promethazine with codeine/"purple drank").

**A heroin (diamorphine) or other opiate/opioid addict may use codeine to ward off the effects of withdrawal during periods where their preferred drug is unavailable or unaffordable.**

### Fatal Overdose Blood Levels

**Blood or plasma codeine concentrations are typically in the 50–300 μg/L range in persons taking the drug therapeutically, 700–7,000 μg/L in chronic users, and 1,000–10,000 μg/L in cases of acute fatal over dosage.**
