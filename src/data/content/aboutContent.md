**dose.wiki** compiles knowledge about psychoactive drugs into one open dataset, readable here or as a download. It holds four collections: **{{compoundCount}}** [substance articles](/substances), **{{effectCount}}** [subjective effect articles](/effects), **{{replicationCount}}** [replications](/replications), and **{{reportCount}}** [trip reports](/reports).

A substance article covers dose, duration, effects, interactions, chemistry, and legality by country. An effect article describes one component of an altered state, from visual drifting to ego death. A replication is an image, video, or sound that reproduces an effect, made by an artist who has experienced it. A trip report is one person's account of one experience.

Much of the information a substance article needs has already been gathered and published: decades of documentation by scientists, psychonauts, and the harm-reduction community. But it is spread primarily across [ten psychopharmacology reference sites](/docs/how#sources) in ten formats, with no shared standard and no one site covering everything. We know what compiling it by hand takes, because we have done it: [Josie Kins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josie_Kins), who co-founded dose.wiki, founded [PsychonautWiki](https://psychonautwiki.rip), the Subjective Effect Index, and numerous other similar projects. PsychonautWiki's first draft took her and a team of contributors from 2011 to 2016. Each article was days of work, each writer set a different standard, and the method was never written down, so every article depended on who wrote it.

Compiling is now what software does well, so substance articles begin as pipeline drafts. Code scrapes and copies the dose and duration tables from the source sites. An LLM reads the same sources and drafts the narrative sections from word-for-word excerpts, citing the source of each one. Human editors take over from there: they verify the pharmacology, check the doses and interactions where a wrong figure can hurt someone, and add what the sources left out. Each article's status subsection shows how much human review it has had, and [How articles are made](/docs/how) shows every step and every prompt.

The shared structure makes information from different sources easier to compare, and makes gaps and disagreements easier to see. Editors build on it with corrections, references, and explanations of their own. Each article links back to the source pages that informed it.

The other three collections are made by people. The effect articles are the Subjective Effect Index, written by contributors over more than a decade across several websites, and dose.wiki is its current home. Replications are made by artists trying to show what an experience looked like from the inside, most of them from the [r/replications](https://reddit.com/r/replications) community on Reddit. Trip reports are written by their authors and reviewed by an editor before they are published.

We dedicate our own contributions to the public domain through [CC0](/docs/license) so others can use and improve them, including our article writing and the schema behind the dataset. Material that belongs to other authors keeps its own terms, including quoted passages, replications, legacy trip reports, the TripSit interaction data, and reagent test data. The site's code is MIT. You can download the datasets on the [Open Data](#data) tab, use them in your own project, or build another encyclopedia on top of them. We want that to happen: whoever builds the next one should start from this dataset instead of from zero.

dose.wiki is in **beta**. The database is live and growing, coverage is incomplete, and most substance articles are still pipeline drafts awaiting human review. Structure and features will keep changing on the way to 1.0. If something looks wrong or missing, tell us through the report-an-issue box at the bottom of any article, the [feedback form](/about/feedback), or <contact@dose.wiki>. An editor reads each message, and every correction fixes the article and the next export of the dataset.
